# Factors That Affect Firearm Suppressor Performance

Scope note: explanatory level only (no dimensions or manufacturing guidance). Research date November 2026. Source-quality flags used below: **[primary]** = lab/standards/peer-reviewed or instrumented test data; **[mfr]** = manufacturer claim; **[press]** = firearms press; **[retail]** = retailer/vendor blog (treat numbers as rough); **[snippet]** = taken from a search-result summary, page not opened in full, so attribution to the exact page is less certain.

Baseline numbers useful for every section:
- Suppressors reduced peak sound pressure level at the shooter's ear by 17-24 dB and 8-hour-equivalent A-weighted energy by 9-21 dB, depending on firearm and ammunition; at an instructor position about 1 m behind the shooter the reductions were 20-28 dB peak and 11-26 dB LAeq,8h. Measured sound power reductions across all tests ranged from 2 to 23 dB. **[primary]** — [Murphy et al., NIOSH/CDC, Int. J. Audiology](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/211059)
- Decibels are logarithmic: +10 dB = 10x intensity, +20 dB = 100x intensity; reference pressure 20 micropascals. **[press]** — [TFB Silencer Saturday #331](https://www.thefirearmblog.com/blog/2024/06/01/suppressor-sound-science/)

## 1. Suppressor-side factors (volume, length/diameter, bore aperture, baffles, materials, mounts, end caps, modular configs)

### Takeaway
Internal volume and how effectively the baffle stack delays and cools gas dominate; a bigger/longer can is quieter (roughly 6 dB at the ear between long and short configurations of one modular can in one independent test), and an over-sized bore (e.g. .30 can on 5.56) costs muzzle suppression but lowers back pressure, which can partly pay it back at the shooter's ear on gas guns. Materials mostly affect weight and heat/erosion durability, not sound.

### Cited Findings
**Volume and basic mechanism**
- A suppressor works by giving gas an expansion volume, acting as a heat sink, and using baffles to create turbulence that delays gas exit (more time for expansion and heat transfer). Rule of thumb quoted from Dr. Philip Dater: the bullet should exit into a tube "ideally 20 times the volume of the gun's bore." **[press, expert]** — [Small Arms Defense Journal, Dr. Dater's LMO Suppressor Class](https://sadefensejournal.com/dr-daters-lmo-suppressor-class/2)
- The baffle stack (shape and arrangement) matters more than the tube. Many geometries exist (K, M, cone, pinched cone, slant, Omega); none is inherently superior. Among six brands tested in the class, any could come out best on a given day, which Dater attributed to atmospheric variation. Four contemporary 5.56 duty cans (KAC M4 QD, SureFire 556K, Gemtech G5, AAC M4-2000) all metered around 135 dB, within 3 dB of each other. **[press, expert]** — [SADJ](https://sadefensejournal.com/dr-daters-lmo-suppressor-class/2)
- Integral suppressors (ported barrel inside a shroud) are "generally quieter" than muzzle cans and add no length, but bleed off considerable velocity. **[press, expert]** — [SADJ](https://sadefensejournal.com/dr-daters-lmo-suppressor-class/2)

**Length / modular long vs short**
- SilencerCo Omega 36M on a .300 Win Mag Tikka T3 (Federal GMM 190 gr): 132.7 dB average at the right ear in long configuration vs 138.7 dB in short configuration, a gap of about 6 dB. The reviewer noted that in most cases the short versions did not reduce noise to "hearing safe" levels. **[press, instrumented]** — [American Rifleman, Tested: SilencerCo Omega 36M](https://www.americanrifleman.org/content/tested-silencerco-omega-36m-suppressor/)
- Same can, long config, manufacturer averages by cartridge: 123.3 dB (9mm), 130.6 dB (5.56), 136.9 dB (.308), 141.4 dB (.338 Lapua). Shows how much the cartridge outweighs the can: about 18 dB spread on one suppressor. **[mfr][snippet]** — [Silencer Central product page / SilencerCo specs](https://www.silencercentral.com/products/silencerco-omega-36m)
- Distance from end cap to the shooter's head matters independently of baffle count: PEW Science found the short Rugged Razor556 produced a muzzle Suppression Rating on par with the much larger SureFire SOCOM762-RC2 on an MK18 with significantly fewer baffles, but the longer can places the end-cap orifice further from the shooter's head (better at-ear result). **[primary]** — [PEW Science SSS.6.76](https://pewscience.com/sound-signature-reviews-free/sss-6-76-rugged-razor556-mk18-556)

**Bore aperture ("over-bore")**
- Controlled comparison: the Otter Creek Labs Polonium-30 is structurally identical to the 5.56/6mm Polonium except for bore aperture. On a 10.3-in MK18 with XM193, the Polonium-30 scored Suppression Rating 30.9 composite, 29.5 muzzle, 23.9 at ear. Versus the tighter-bore Polonium its bystander (muzzle) rating is lower, while its at-ear rating is "somewhat higher": larger bore = lower back pressure and higher flow rate = less ejection-port blast, but less controlled combustion and more severe muzzle blast/jetting. PEW places it a performance class below most dedicated 5.56 cans (Polonium, SOCOM556-RC2, CAT WB). **[primary]** — [PEW Science SSS.6.143](https://pewscience.com/sound-signature-reviews-free/sss-6-143-ocl-polonium-30-mk18-556)
- PEW's Rugged Razor testing: over-bore "may result in sound suppression performance degradation on 5.56x45mm platforms with some designs"; swapping to a 5.56 end cap suppressed first-shot blast noticeably more than the .30 end cap. So a caliber-matched end cap recovers part of the loss. **[primary][snippet]** — [PEW Science SSS.6.59](https://pewscience.com/sound-signature-reviews-members-only/sss-6-59-research-supplement-rugged-razor-556-endcap-mk18)
- Retailer consensus: a caliber-matched can generally delivers better sound and flash reduction than a "universal" larger-bore can, and is usually lighter. **[retail][snippet]** — [Silencer Central](https://www.silencercentral.com/blog/can-you-use-a-30-caliber-suppressor-with-556/)

**Muzzle device / mount interaction**
- SureFire states the SOCOM556-RC3 may show more flash with closed-tine adapters and muzzle brakes; gas flow in flow-optimised cans is highly sensitive to small changes in the muzzle device. **[press, citing mfr]** — [TFB Silencer Saturday #308](https://www.thefirearmblog.com/blog/2023/12/16/surefire-socom556-rc3/)
- Brands differ in muzzle overhang and point-of-impact shift, which Dater says matter more in practice than small sound differences. **[press, expert]** — [SADJ](https://sadefensejournal.com/dr-daters-lmo-suppressor-class/2)

**Materials (affect durability/weight, not sound directly)**
- Listed maximum service temperatures / tensile strengths: 7075 aluminium 212 F, 83,000 psi (strength collapses around 250 F); Ti-6Al-4V 660 F, 160,000 psi (by 600 F strength can fall below some aluminium alloys); 17-4 stainless 600 F, 190,000 psi; Inconel 718 1300 F, 150,000 psi with strength curves nearly flat to about 1400 F. Recommendation: titanium when weight matters and high rates of fire are not expected; 17-4 or Inconel for short barrels and heavy fire. **[press]** — [TFB Silencer Saturday #329](https://www.thefirearmblog.com/blog/2024/05/18/suppressor-alloy-metallurgy/)
- Inconel is commonly used for the blast baffle (first baffle) for erosion resistance at high temperature; Stellite is cited as tougher still. Sources conflict on 17-4: one maker says it shows the most wear resistance in its testing, another source says its lower erosion resistance makes it less suited to high-volume/full-auto use. **[mfr/retail][snippet]** — [Griffin Armament](https://griffinarmament.com/choosing-a-suppressor-material-construction); [TTAG](https://www.thetruthaboutguns.com/best-metals-for-suppressors)

### Inferences
- For a game model: treat "can size class" (K / standard / long) as the main suppressor-side sound stat (about 3-6 dB per step), bore mismatch as a small penalty at the muzzle (order of a few dB; no clean number found) traded against reduced back-pressure penalties, and material as a weight/heat-capacity/durability stat with no direct sound effect.
- Because well-designed duty cans of the same class meter within about 3 dB of each other, differences between cans of the same size and bore are smaller than differences caused by host, barrel length, and ammunition.

### Gaps
- No head-to-head dB figure found for .30 can vs dedicated 5.56 can (PEW gives ratings, not dB, and the comparison chart is an image); no data found for a 9mm can on .22 LR.
- No quantitative source found isolating diameter from length, or baffle count in isolation.
- No instrumented source found comparing direct-thread vs quick-detach mounts for sound. Unsourced general understanding: mount type mainly affects weight, length, repeatability of point-of-impact shift, and risk of loosening/carbon-lock, not sound.
- Materials' effect on tone ("ring" of titanium vs steel) is widely discussed anecdotally; no reliable source found.

## 2. Host-side factors (barrel length, action type, gas system, ejection port, muzzle vs ear)

### Takeaway
The host weapon changes results as much as the suppressor: shorter barrels raise muzzle pressure and blast, and any self-loading action that opens while the suppressor is still pressurised vents noise and gas at the ejection port, so manually operated or locked actions are the quietest at the shooter's ear and the right ear of an AR shooter gets far less benefit than a 1 m muzzle measurement suggests.

### Cited Findings
**Barrel length**
- SureFire-published at-ear data for the SOCOM556-RC2: 137.74 dB on a 10.3-in barrel vs 136.40 dB on a 14.5-in barrel (about 1.3 dB). Muzzle pressure difference between 11.5-in and 14.5-in barrels is about 2,000 psi. **[press citing mfr]** — [TFB Silencer Saturday #308](https://www.thefirearmblog.com/blog/2023/12/16/surefire-socom556-rc3/)
- Unsuppressed, a 10.3-in 5.56 produces a primary blast "coincidentally similar in peak pressure amplitude, positive phase duration, and wave shape" to a 20-in 7.62x51; the 14.5-in 5.56 is slightly lower in both amplitude and duration. Short 5.56 barrels also produce a significant secondary blast (external re-ignition of unburnt propellant) whose peak is "almost as severe as the subsonic 300 BLK primary blast wave" from an 8-in barrel. **[primary]** — [PEW Science SSS.6.127](https://pewscience.com/sound-signature-reviews-free/sss-6-127-research-supplement-unsuppressed-rifle-and-m4a1)
- Suppressor rankings do not transfer across barrel lengths: suppressed signatures on 10.3-in and 14.5-in hosts may differ for each silencer and relative performance "may not scale linearly." **[primary]** — [PEW Science SSS.6.127](https://pewscience.com/sound-signature-reviews-free/sss-6-127-research-supplement-unsuppressed-rifle-and-m4a1)
- Study of nine AR-15 rifles and 14 suppressors: suppressors brought levels to 140 dB peak or below in only a subset of AR-15 conditions; shorter barrel length and muzzle-brake devices substantially increase user exposure. **[primary][snippet]** — [Lobarinas et al., PubMed 26821935](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26821935/)
- Shorter barrels react more sensitively to suppressor back pressure and show larger functional deviations. **[mfr technical]** — [B&T know-how](https://bt-ag.ch/en/know-how/blowback-backpressure-rbs/)
- The same can measures very differently by host cartridge/barrel: Polonium-30 rated 30.9 on a 10.3-in 5.56 MK18 but 37.1 on a 20-in .308 bolt action. **[primary]** — [PEW Science SSS.6.143](https://pewscience.com/sound-signature-reviews-free/sss-6-143-ocl-polonium-30-mk18-556)

**Action type and ejection-port noise**
- On AR-15s, peak reductions from suppressors were 20-25 dB at the 1 m position, 15-25 dB at the left ear, but only 6-9 dB at the right ear, attributed to gas venting from the ejection port on the right side. **[primary][snippet]** — [Murphy et al. summarising Lobarinas, CDC stacks](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/211059)
- Manually operated repeaters (bolt, straight-pull, lever) are not affected in function by a suppressor and produce no blowback, because the breech stays closed. **[mfr technical]** — [B&T know-how](https://bt-ag.ch/en/know-how/blowback-backpressure-rbs/)
- Bolt-action example: SilencerCo Omega 300 on a 20-in .308 bolt rifle has an at-ear rating "significantly higher" (quieter) than its muzzle rating, i.e. the shooter is better off than bystanders beside the muzzle. On gas guns the relationship can invert (Polonium-30 on MK18: ear 23.9 vs muzzle 29.5). **[primary]** — [PEW Science Suppression Rating](https://pewscience.com/silencer-sound-standard-suppression-rating); [PEW SSS.6.143](https://pewscience.com/sound-signature-reviews-free/sss-6-143-ocl-polonium-30-mk18-556)
- Muzzle blast and ejection-port blast "coalesce" at the ear. High-back-pressure cans may produce more hazardous port blast on a 14.5-in mid-length gas system than on a 10.3-in carbine system, because of dwell-time differences. **[primary]** — [PEW Science SSS.6.127](https://pewscience.com/sound-signature-reviews-free/sss-6-127-research-supplement-unsuppressed-rifle-and-m4a1)
- Mechanical action noise sets a floor: an M4 bolt closing on an empty chamber meters 117 dB. PEW traces show bolt-closing noise as a late event about 80 ms after the shot. **[press, expert]** — [SADJ](https://sadefensejournal.com/dr-daters-lmo-suppressor-class/2); **[primary]** — [PEW SSS.6.143](https://pewscience.com/sound-signature-reviews-free/sss-6-143-ocl-polonium-30-mk18-556)
- Blowback pistols/SMGs rely only on slide/bolt mass and spring; suppressed, they vent sooty gas through the slide gap and ejection port toward the shooter. A suppressed blowback 9mm SMG prototype tested indoors produced enough blowback to effectively blind both shooters after a few bursts. **[retail][snippet]** — [Capitol Armory pistol suppressor guide](https://www.capitolarmory.com/articles/pistol-suppressor-guide); **[press]** — [TFB](https://www.thefirearmblog.com/blog/low-back-pressure-suppressors-do-they-really-make-a-difference-44817520)
- A slide lock (as on the Vietnam-era "Hush Puppy" pistol) eliminates slide-cycling noise and ejection-port gas noise at the cost of manual cycling. **[press][snippet]** — [Athlon Outdoors, Hush Puppy](https://athlonoutdoors.com/article/hush-puppy-seal-silencer-system/)
- Captive-piston ammunition (Soviet SP-4) seals gas in the case and needs no suppressor: 124.6 dB, about equal to an integrally suppressed .22 LR Ruger Mk II. **[press, expert]** — [SADJ](https://sadefensejournal.com/dr-daters-lmo-suppressor-class/2)
- Shotguns are described as not effectively suppressible with conventional baffle stacks. **[press, expert]** — [SADJ](https://sadefensejournal.com/dr-daters-lmo-suppressor-class/2) (note: commercial shotgun suppressors do exist; this reflects the lecturer's view at the time)

**Gas system and tuning**
- A suppressor does not raise peak pressure; it lengthens the pressure curve, giving the action surplus energy ("overfunction"). Gas and blowback actions are designed around about 5-6 m/s bolt velocity. AR ejection angle is a visible indicator: about 120-130 degrees unsuppressed, 45-50 degrees with a conventional can, about 90 degrees with a reduced-back-pressure can. **[mfr technical]** — [B&T know-how](https://bt-ag.ch/en/know-how/blowback-backpressure-rbs/)
- Adjustable gas blocks reduce overfunction but do not correct blowback, because residual pressure in the can outlasts the time until the bolt opens. A 10% increase in function can cause a 50-100% increase in blowback. **[mfr technical]** — [B&T know-how](https://bt-ag.ch/en/know-how/blowback-backpressure-rbs/)
- Barrel length, gas-system length, ammunition, and buffer weight all change suppressed behaviour. PEW's reference M4A1 is specified down to gas port size, H2 buffer and spring for this reason. **[press]** — [TFB #308](https://www.thefirearmblog.com/blog/2023/12/16/surefire-socom556-rc3/); **[primary]** — [PEW SSS.6.127](https://pewscience.com/sound-signature-reviews-free/sss-6-127-research-supplement-unsuppressed-rifle-and-m4a1)
- Suppressed ARs vent gas back through the charging handle; gas-sealing charging handles are a common mitigation. **[press, expert]** — [SADJ](https://sadefensejournal.com/dr-daters-lmo-suppressor-class/2)

### Inferences
- Ranking of hosts by suppressed at-ear quietness (for the same cartridge): manually operated / locked-slide > long-barrel gas gun > short-barrel gas gun > simple blowback. The muzzle-side (bystander/enemy) ranking depends mainly on barrel length and cartridge, not action.
- For a game, "sound heard by others" should key off the muzzle signature (barrel length, cartridge, can), while "penalty to the shooter" (gas, fatigue, hearing) should key off action type and back pressure.
- The roughly 1.3 dB at-ear difference between 10.3 and 14.5 in understates the muzzle-side difference, since short barrels add secondary blast and flash.

### Gaps
- No reliable sourced figures found for revolver cylinder-gap leakage (the general understanding that conventional revolvers leak blast at the gap and are poor suppressor hosts, with gas-seal designs such as the Nagant M1895 as exceptions, was not verified in a source this session).
- No source retrieved explaining recoil boosters (Nielsen devices) for tilting-barrel pistols; general understanding is they affect cycling reliability, not sound.
- No instrumented direct-impingement vs piston comparison found. No 5.56 dB data for 16-20 in barrels on the same suppressor.
- PEW's numeric peak/impulse data by barrel length are in images or members-only.

## 3. Ammunition factors

### Takeaway
A suppressor only treats muzzle blast; a supersonic bullet's ballistic crack is untouched, so subsonic ammunition is the single largest ammunition lever, and cartridges with small powder charges and low muzzle pressure (.22 LR, 9mm 147 gr, .45 ACP, subsonic .300 BLK) are inherently easy to suppress. Published dB numbers for specific cartridges are mostly vendor figures and conflict.

### Cited Findings
- Suppressor sound-power reductions are larger with low-velocity than with high-velocity ammunition in the same firearms, because supersonic bullets generate N-waves the suppressor cannot affect. **[primary]** — [Murphy et al., CDC](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/211059)
- Gunshot sound has two sources: propellant gas (including unburnt powder igniting outside the barrel) and the bullet's supersonic shock. Ammunition loaded for the host barrel length (less unburnt powder at the muzzle) reduces the first; subsonic ammunition removes the second. **[press]** — [TFB #331](https://www.thefirearmblog.com/blog/2024/06/01/suppressor-sound-science/)
- Factory subsonic loads are generally loaded to 1,000-1,050 fps; shot-to-shot velocity spread can reach 50 fps, so some loads in some guns occasionally go supersonic. **[press]** — [American Rifleman, Subsonic Ammo 101](https://www.americanrifleman.org/content/subsonic-ammo-101-everything-the-suppressor-shooter-should-know/)
- Trade-off: most rifle bullets need impact above about 1,600 fps for meaningful expansion, so subsonic rifle loads need purpose-designed bullets; pistol defensive loads still perform at subsonic velocity. Subsonic loads often produce less pressure and can fail to cycle semi-autos (fix: gas-block adjustment on ARs, different recoil springs on pistols). **[press]** — [American Rifleman](https://www.americanrifleman.org/content/subsonic-ammo-101-everything-the-suppressor-shooter-should-know/)
- .300 BLK guns tuned for conventional cans often under-function with a reduced-back-pressure can plus subsonic ammunition. **[mfr technical]** — [B&T know-how](https://bt-ag.ch/en/know-how/blowback-backpressure-rbs/)
- .22 LR is called ideal to suppress because of its favourable suppressor-to-bore volume ratio and low starting signature; an integrally suppressed .22 pistol is in the mid-120s dB. **[press, expert]** — [SADJ](https://sadefensejournal.com/dr-daters-lmo-suppressor-class/2)
- .22 LR reaches peak velocity at about 16 in of barrel; some .22 loads sit right at the speed of sound, where roughly 25 fps can tip them supersonic. **[press]** — [American Rifleman](https://www.americanrifleman.org/content/subsonic-ammo-101-everything-the-suppressor-shooter-should-know/); **[press][snippet]** — [TTAG](https://thetruthaboutguns.com/ammo-review-american-eagle-suppressor-45-acp)
- Hotter ammunition shows a bigger wet-vs-dry difference (more gas energy for the coolant to absorb). **[retail/press][snippet]** — [TTAG, shooting a suppressor wet](https://www.thetruthaboutguns.com/silencers-for-beginners-shooting-a-suppressor-wet/amp/)
- Vendor ballparks (low confidence, methods unstated): suppressed subsonic .300 BLK from a 16-in barrel 120-130 dB; suppressed .45 ACP about 129-137 dB (standard 230 gr is already subsonic); suppressed 9mm about 120-125 dB in one comparison. **[retail][snippet]** — [Silencer Shop](https://www.silencershop.com/blog/subsonic-ammo-vs-supersonic-ammo); [GunMag Warehouse](https://gunmagwarehouse.com/blog/are-suppressors-really-whisper-quiet/)
- Conflicts: one vendor puts suppressed supersonic .308 at 165-170 dB and suppressed supersonic 9mm at about 160 dB, which contradicts manufacturer and independent at-ear data in the 130s (e.g. 136.9 dB .308 above); one vendor claims the crack is 30-50% of suppressed noise. American Rifleman states suppressed subsonic can be "as low as about 70 dB," which is implausible against every instrumented figure here (bolt closing alone is 117 dB). Treat all three as unreliable. **[retail][snippet]** — [Popular Suppressors](https://popularsuppressors.com/articles/subsonic-vs-supersonic-suppressor-ammo/); [American Rifleman](https://www.americanrifleman.org/content/subsonic-ammo-101-everything-the-suppressor-shooter-should-know/)

### Inferences
- Cartridge energy sets the floor: on one suppressor, manufacturer numbers span about 123 dB (9mm) to 141 dB (.338 LM). A reasonable model is suppressed loudness = cartridge/barrel baseline minus suppressor reduction, plus a separate "supersonic crack" flag that the suppressor cannot remove and that is heard along the bullet's flight path rather than at the shooter.
- Heavy-for-calibre bullets (147 gr 9mm, 190-220 gr .300 BLK, 230 gr .45) are how subsonic loads retain energy; the cost is range, trajectory, and sometimes cycling.

### Gaps
- No instrumented dB level for the ballistic crack itself was found; no measured data for 9x39mm (TFB has a 9x39 vs .300 BLK ballistics series, but no sound data was retrieved).
- No quantified source on powder burn rate vs suppressed sound. General understanding (unsourced here): fast powders that burn fully in the barrel give lower muzzle pressure and less secondary flash, which is why .300 BLK suits short barrels.
- No explicit sourced velocities for 147 gr 9mm or 230 gr .45 ACP retrieved this session.

## 4. Environmental factors

### Takeaway
Temperature is the main environmental variable because it sets the speed of sound (about 1,047 fps at -4 F to about 1,170 fps at 104 F), which decides whether a borderline "subsonic" load cracks; humidity and altitude are second-order. Reflections (indoor ranges, walls, even the shooter's own head) change measured and perceived levels substantially.

### Cited Findings
- Speed of sound: about 1,087 fps at 32 F and about 1,125 fps at 68 F at sea level; 68 F with 75% humidity about 1,128 fps (humidity adds only about 3 fps). **[press]** — [American Rifleman](https://www.americanrifleman.org/content/subsonic-ammo-101-everything-the-suppressor-shooter-should-know/)
- Table range: 1,047 fps at -4 F / 0% humidity to 1,170.5 fps at 104 F / 100% humidity (about 123 fps span). 0 to 100% humidity at 68 F changes it by about 4 fps. Standard-atmosphere altitude from 0 to 10,000 ft lowers it by about 39 fps, mainly because air is colder at altitude; density itself plays a lesser role. **[press]** — [TFB #331](https://www.thefirearmblog.com/blog/2024/06/01/suppressor-sound-science/)
- Sources disagree on altitude in isolation: Denver at 32 F is given as about 1,086 fps vs 1,087 fps at sea level (effectively no change at equal temperature). **[press]** — [American Rifleman](https://www.americanrifleman.org/content/subsonic-ammo-101-everything-the-suppressor-shooter-should-know/)
- Cold also lowers muzzle velocity slightly and heat raises it, so hot weather pushes borderline loads supersonic from both directions, but the speed of sound rises faster than typical velocity gains. **[retail][snippet]** — [Popular Suppressors](https://popularsuppressors.com/?p=36)
- Atmospheric conditions shift results enough to reorder similar suppressors from day to day. **[press, expert]** — [SADJ](https://sadefensejournal.com/dr-daters-lmo-suppressor-class/2)
- PEW Science tests indoors and outdoors and studied reflections, temperature, ambient noise, wind and electromagnetic interference; it reports waveforms on time scales "showing no reflection influence." Reflections from the shooter's own head amplify and time-shift the at-ear signal, so free-field muzzle data and near-reflector ear data must not be compared directly. **[primary]** — [PEW Science test method](https://pewscience.com/silencer-sound-standard-test-method); [PEW Suppression Rating](https://pewscience.com/silencer-sound-standard-suppression-rating)
- Indoors, back pressure effects are worse: gas cloud lingers, obstructs vision (especially under night vision) and can induce flinch. **[press]** — [TFB](https://www.thefirearmblog.com/blog/low-back-pressure-suppressors-do-they-really-make-a-difference-44817520)
- Distance attenuation illustration: a 170 dB source falls to about 130 dB by 96 inches in the article's table (conditions unstated). **[press]** — [TFB #331](https://www.thefirearmblog.com/blog/2024/06/01/suppressor-sound-science/)

### Inferences
- A 1,050 fps load is safely subsonic at 68 F (margin about 75 fps) but has almost no margin near 0 F (speed of sound about 1,050 fps); with up to 50 fps velocity spread, cold environments can make "subsonic" ammunition crack intermittently. This is a clean, realistic game mechanic for cold maps.
- Indoor/hard-surfaced environments should raise both perceived loudness and gas-related shooter penalties; open terrain is the best case.

### Gaps
- No quantified source found for how much indoor reflection adds in dB to suppressed fire, or for air density's effect on suppressor reduction itself (as opposed to speed of sound).
- No source found on rain/water in the bore or suppressor beyond deliberate "wet" use.

## 5. Usage factors (first round pop, wet vs dry, heat, fouling, wear, ratings)

### Takeaway
The first shot through a cool, air-filled suppressor is louder (first round pop) because oxygen inside supports secondary combustion; adding a little liquid or gel ("wet") gives roughly 3-10 dB extra reduction for the first several rounds. Sustained fire is governed by material heat limits, and "full-auto rated" and minimum-barrel-length ratings are manufacturer-defined, not standardised.

### Cited Findings
- First round pop (FRP): the first round meters higher because superheated, incompletely burned powder ignites in the oxygen-rich suppressor; later shots fire into combustion gas and are quieter. **[press, expert]** — [SADJ](https://sadefensejournal.com/dr-daters-lmo-suppressor-class/2); **[retail]** — [Capitol Armory](https://capitolarmory.com/blog/what-is-first-round-pop.html)
- FRP is not universal in direction over a string: on the Polonium-30 the first shot was more hazardous than the second, but later shots became more severe than the first, on both a 5.56 MK18 and a .308 bolt action. PEW records six shots and analyses five. **[primary]** — [PEW Science SSS.6.143](https://pewscience.com/sound-signature-reviews-free/sss-6-143-ocl-polonium-30-mk18-556)
- Bore/end-cap size affects FRP: a 5.56 end cap on a .30 can suppressed first-shot blast noticeably more than the .30 end cap. **[primary][snippet]** — [PEW Science SSS.6.59](https://pewscience.com/sound-signature-reviews-members-only/sss-6-59-research-supplement-rugged-razor-556-endcap-mk18)
- Wet vs dry: an ablative (water, gel, grease) typically adds 3-10 dB of reduction for the first several rounds until it burns off. There is no industry standard for wet testing, so wet numbers are not comparable across brands; Dater notes some makers have rigged tests by wetting a dry can. **[retail/press][snippet]** — [EDC Index suppressor guide](https://edc-index.pages.dev/suppressor-guide); [TTAG](https://www.thetruthaboutguns.com/silencers-for-beginners-shooting-a-suppressor-wet/amp/); **[press, expert]** — [SADJ](https://sadefensejournal.com/dr-daters-lmo-suppressor-class/2)
- A commercial suppressor foam averaged 3-5 dB reduction over 10-shot strings, with the biggest effect on the first shot, sometimes exceeding 10 dB (product-linked testing; treat with caution). **[press, mfr-sourced][snippet]** — [TFB](https://www.thefirearmblog.com/blog/?p=658676)
- Shot-to-shot consistency: dB readings in Dater's class varied by less than 2-3 dB while velocities varied by up to 200 fps. **[press, expert]** — [SADJ](https://sadefensejournal.com/dr-daters-lmo-suppressor-class/2)
- Heat limits by material (see section 1): aluminium loses strength near 250 F, titanium by about 600 F, Inconel holds to about 1300-1400 F. **[press]** — [TFB #329](https://www.thefirearmblog.com/blog/2024/05/18/suppressor-alloy-metallurgy/)
- "Full-auto rated" has no common definition: it depends on each maker's firing schedule; a rating might mean five rounds or 300, and some are limited (e.g. 90 rounds then cool). The underlying question is whether the can survives without baffle erosion. **[press/retail][snippet]** — [MeatEater](https://www.themeateater.com/hunt/firearm-hunting/all-your-suppressor-questions-answered)
- Minimum barrel-length ratings interact with material: one manufacturer's chart rates a titanium core "light/limited" duty on 5.56 at 10.3 in and over and semi-auto only below that, while the Inconel core is rated heavy/SOCOM duty at 10.3 in and over and light/limited below. **[mfr][snippet]** — [Precision Armament TiTrex 300Ti](https://precisionarmament.com/titrex-300ti-suppressor/)
- Lower muzzle pressure (longer barrel, lower-pressure can design) extends suppressor life. **[press citing mfr]** — [TFB #308](https://www.thefirearmblog.com/blog/2023/12/16/surefire-socom556-rc3/)
- Fouling on the host: high back pressure deposits carbon in the action sooner, so a gun that runs thousands of rounds unsuppressed chokes earlier suppressed, especially in dust or humidity; fouled receivers also change bolt timing. **[press]** — [TFB](https://www.thefirearmblog.com/blog/low-back-pressure-suppressors-do-they-really-make-a-difference-44817520); **[primary]** — [PEW SSS.6.143](https://pewscience.com/sound-signature-reviews-free/sss-6-143-ocl-polonium-30-mk18-556)
- Dater's position is that a (centerfire) suppressor should never be cleaned. **[press, expert]** — [SADJ](https://sadefensejournal.com/dr-daters-lmo-suppressor-class/2)

### Inferences
- FRP matters most for a single-shot stealth scenario: the one shot that matters is the loudest one, unless the can is wet. A consumable "wet the can" action giving roughly 3-10 dB for the first few shots is well supported.
- Heat and durability should scale with barrel shortness, cartridge pressure and rate of fire; lightweight (titanium/aluminium) cans should carry rate-of-fire or barrel-length restrictions.

### Gaps
- No instrumented source found quantifying how a hot suppressor changes sound level, or the magnitude of FRP in dB for typical dry cans (commonly quoted figures of a few dB were not found in a citable primary source this session).
- No quantified source on carbon/lead fouling changing sound or weight (rimfire lead build-up is well known anecdotally), nor on baffle-erosion rates or service life in rounds, apart from one manufacturer representative's statement of cans exceeding 18,000 rounds without issues ([TFB](https://www.thefirearmblog.com/blog/low-back-pressure-suppressors-do-they-really-make-a-difference-44817520)).
- Mirage from a hot suppressor disturbing the sight picture is commonly reported but not sourced here.

## 6. Trade-offs (back pressure vs suppression, size/weight, durability, reliability, accuracy, velocity)

### Takeaway
The core trade is trapping gas (quiet muzzle, high back pressure) versus letting it flow (lower back pressure, less port noise and gas to the shooter, usually a louder muzzle). Conventional cans can raise cyclic rate by roughly 20% or up to about 200 rpm; flow-through designs bring it back near unsuppressed. Velocity changes are small (tens of fps), while point-of-impact shift is the main accuracy consequence.

### Cited Findings
**Back pressure vs sound**
- Bypass / reduced-back-pressure designs are often louder at the muzzle (bypass flow is slowed less than core flow) but can be quieter at the shooter's ear because less gas exits at the ejection port ("port pop"). **[mfr technical]** — [B&T know-how](https://bt-ag.ch/en/know-how/blowback-backpressure-rbs/)
- Same pattern from bore size alone: the over-bore Polonium-30 has a lower muzzle rating but a somewhat higher at-ear rating than its tight-bore sibling on an MK18. **[primary]** — [PEW Science SSS.6.143](https://pewscience.com/sound-signature-reviews-free/sss-6-143-ocl-polonium-30-mk18-556)
- SureFire's SOCOM556-RC3 claims 60% less back pressure than the RC2 at the same weight (17 oz) and nearly the same length; a reviewer judged it about 2 dB louder than the RC2 on a 14.5-in host by ear and indistinguishable on an 11.5-in host. The benefit is largest on short barrels. **[press citing mfr; by-ear estimate]** — [TFB #308](https://www.thefirearmblog.com/blog/2023/12/16/surefire-socom556-rc3/)
- Back pressure covers total gas to the rear, gas in the shooter's face, and effects on cyclic rate and bolt speed; flow rate is inferred from pressure drop. **[mfr][snippet]** — [Thunder Beast Arms back pressure method](https://thunderbeastarms.com/tech/backpressure/)

**Cyclic rate, reliability, wear**
- Suppressors can raise full-auto cyclic rate by up to 200 rounds per minute. **[press, expert]** — [SADJ](https://sadefensejournal.com/dr-daters-lmo-suppressor-class/2)
- Full-auto test: 847 rpm unsuppressed, about 23% higher with a conventional setup, back to about 850 rpm with a flow-through can. **[press, mfr-linked][snippet]** — [Athlon Outdoors, MAXFLO 3D](https://athlonoutdoors.com/article/ng2-maxflo-3d-suppressor/)
- Instrumented barrel data for two low-back-pressure cans: 50% back-pressure increase with 5% cyclic-rate increase vs 48% and 3%. **[press citing mfr][snippet]** — [Athlon Outdoors, TBAC Spiro](https://athlonoutdoors.com/article/thunderbeasts-new-spiro-suppressor/)
- Higher bolt-carrier velocity breaks parts designed for lower stress and accelerates fouling; reviewer found high-back-pressure cans noticeably reduced reliability in dusty or humid conditions. **[press]** — [TFB](https://www.thefirearmblog.com/blog/low-back-pressure-suppressors-do-they-really-make-a-difference-44817520)
- Gas to the face: blowback is measured in a NATO-style closed box with a gas probe at the shooter's nose position. Adjustable gas reduces overfunction but not blowback. **[mfr technical]** — [B&T know-how](https://bt-ag.ch/en/know-how/blowback-backpressure-rbs/)

**Size, weight, handling**
- Suppressors add forward weight, slow target transitions and hamper close-quarters handling; they also reduce recoil substantially (Dater calls them "the world's best muzzle brakes"). **[press, expert]** — [SADJ](https://sadefensejournal.com/dr-daters-lmo-suppressor-class/2)
- Short configuration costs about 6 dB at the ear on a magnum rifle (section 1). **[press]** — [American Rifleman](https://www.americanrifleman.org/content/tested-silencerco-omega-36m-suppressor/)
- Durability vs weight: titanium for light weight and low rates of fire; 17-4 / Inconel for short barrels and heavy fire (section 1). **[press]** — [TFB #329](https://www.thefirearmblog.com/blog/2024/05/18/suppressor-alloy-metallurgy/)

**Velocity and accuracy**
- Typical muzzle-velocity change is a gain of about 10-40 fps (some combinations 50+ fps); one cited study found some loads 10-20 fps faster and others 10-30 fps slower suppressed. Sources conflict on sign; magnitude is consistently small. **[retail/press][snippet]** — [Crate Club](https://crateclub.com/blogs/loadout/do-suppressors-increase-velocity-understanding-the-impact-on-muzzle-velocity-and-performance); [Athlon Outdoors](https://athlonoutdoors.com/article/ballistic-app-suppressor-pro-tip/)
- Point of impact almost always shifts (added muzzle mass changes barrel harmonics); often downward but not reliably. Group size usually stays the same or improves. Standard practice is to re-zero suppressed. **[retail][snippet]** — [Creedmoor Sports](https://www.creedmoorsports.com/do-suppressors-affect-accuracy/); [Silencer Shop](https://www.silencershop.com/blog/do-suppressors-affect-accuracy)
- Integral and some pistol-calibre suppressors deliberately bleed velocity to keep supersonic ammunition subsonic. **[press, expert]** — [SADJ](https://sadefensejournal.com/dr-daters-lmo-suppressor-class/2)

### Inferences
- A workable game model has two partly opposed stats per suppressor: muzzle suppression and back pressure. On self-loading hosts, back pressure drives cyclic-rate increase, fouling/malfunction chance, parts wear, gas-in-face penalties (worse indoors) and at-ear noise; on manual actions back pressure is free, so high-back-pressure cans are strictly best there.
- Velocity change can be modelled as negligible (0 to +2%) for muzzle cans and a deliberate loss for integral designs; accuracy effect is a zero shift, not a dispersion penalty.

### Gaps
- No independent, like-for-like dB delta between a flow-through can and a conventional can of equal size was retrieved (PEW's low-back-pressure 5.56 supplement SSS.6.169 exists but is members-only).
- Thunder Beast's back pressure page returned an error; only a snippet was available.
- The "Effects of Sound Suppressors on Muzzle Velocity, Bullet Yaw, and Drag" study is cited second-hand only.

## 7. Measurement factors

### Takeaway
A single "dB" figure hides almost everything: results depend on microphone position (1 m left of muzzle vs 15 cm from the ear), equipment rise time and sampling rate, metric (peak vs energy/impulse-based), host, ammunition, shot count, weather and reflections, so numbers from different sources are not comparable. PEW Science's Suppression Rating is a 0-100 hearing-damage-risk score built from full waveforms at both positions for one specific gun/can/ammo system.

### Cited Findings
- MIL-STD-1474D practice: microphone 1 m to the left of the muzzle at 90 degrees to the bore; classic kit is a Larson Davis 800B meter (20 microsecond rise time) with a quarter-inch pressure microphone (rise time no more than 5 microseconds), calibrated at 114 dB before use. Without correct setup the data are invalid; inflated claims are "specmanship." **[press, expert]** — [SADJ](https://sadefensejournal.com/dr-daters-lmo-suppressor-class/2)
- 1474D firearm rise-time requirement: 1/20 of the A-duration but not more than 20 microseconds. **[equipment mfr][snippet]** — [Larson Davis Firearm Sound Briefing](https://www.larsondavis.com/ContentStore/mktg/LD_Docs/Firearm_Sound_Briefing.pdf)
- Minimum sampling rate: 160,000 samples/s under 1474D vs 192,000 samples/s under 1474E; peak reported as LZpeak (unweighted). **[equipment mfr][snippet]** — [Bruel & Kjaer BP0006](https://www.bksv.com/media/doc/bp0006.pdf)
- 1474E (2015) moved beyond peak level to energy/dose metrics: LIAeq,100ms (A-weighted equivalent energy) and the AHAAH ear model. DoD used 1474D as its de facto damage-risk criterion until 2015. **[primary][snippet]** — [NIH PMC8588962](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8588962)
- Sampling too slowly under-reads: rates around 50 kHz can grossly underestimate true peak; about 200 kHz is recommended best practice. PEW Science samples at 1.0 MS/s. **[primary][snippet]** — [Canadian Audiologist](https://canadianaudiologist.ca/impulse-noise-produced-by-weapons-implications-for-hearing-conservation/?output=pdf); **[primary]** — [PEW SSS.6.143](https://pewscience.com/sound-signature-reviews-free/sss-6-143-ocl-polonium-30-mk18-556)
- Peak and energy metrics disagree: the same suppressors cut peak SPL at the ear by 17-24 dB but A-weighted 8-hour energy by only 9-21 dB. Even suppressed, cumulative exposure remains a hearing risk and hearing protection is recommended. **[primary]** — [Murphy et al., CDC](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/211059)
- Position matters enormously: on AR-15s, reductions were 20-25 dB at 1 m but only 6-9 dB at the right ear. **[primary][snippet]** — [Murphy et al., CDC](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/211059)
- PEW Science Silencer Sound Standard positions: muzzle mic 1.0 m left of muzzle at 1.6 m height (free field); ear mic 0.15 m right of the shooter's right ear with a human shooter present. Five shots per configuration, each recorded individually, raw unfiltered waveforms. Comparing free-field muzzle data with near-head ear data directly is called "dangerous." **[primary]** — [PEW Science test method](https://pewscience.com/silencer-sound-standard-test-method)
- Suppression Rating: 0 (most severe) to 100 (quietest possible small-arm system); accounts for peak pressure and duration at muzzle and ear, phase/frequency content, and hearing-damage potential for shooter and bystanders, mapped in pressure-impulse (P-i) space to hearing damage levels. The headline number is a composite of shooter and bystander estimates. "Each weapon system has a unique Suppression Rating", i.e. the rating belongs to the gun + can + ammo combination, not the suppressor alone. Dose guidance assumes occasional exposure. **[primary]** — [PEW Science Suppression Rating](https://pewscience.com/silencer-sound-standard-suppression-rating)
- Illustration of system dependence: Polonium-30 = 30.9 on a 10.3-in 5.56 MK18, 37.1 on a 20-in .308 bolt gun. PEW also derives a back-pressure metric ("Omega") and cautions that bolt closing time alone is unreliable when the receiver is fouled. **[primary]** — [PEW SSS.6.143](https://pewscience.com/sound-signature-reviews-free/sss-6-143-ocl-polonium-30-mk18-556)
- Press and manufacturer tests vary method: some report LZpeak with the microphone 6 ft away rather than 1 m; manufacturers may quote muzzle or ear, wet or dry, with unspecified hosts. **[press][snippet]** — [TFB](https://www.thefirearmblog.com/blog/pew-science-tests-the-ocl-infinity-556k-44829859); [EDC Index](https://edc-index.pages.dev/suppressor-guide)
- The 140 dB peak figure is the commonly used "hearing safe" impulse threshold; many AR-15 suppressor combinations do not get below it at the ear. **[primary][snippet]** — [Lobarinas et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26821935/); **[press]** — [American Rifleman](https://www.americanrifleman.org/content/subsonic-ammo-101-everything-the-suppressor-shooter-should-know/)

### Inferences
- Only compare numbers taken by the same lab with the same host, ammunition, position and metric. Differences under about 3 dB between suppressors from different tests are within day-to-day and method noise.
- For game design, an abstract 0-100 "suppression" score per weapon+suppressor+ammo system (PEW-style), with separate muzzle (detection by others) and at-ear (shooter effects) components, is more faithful than a fixed per-suppressor dB reduction.
- Rough hierarchy of effect sizes supported by the sources: cartridge and sub/supersonic choice (10-20 dB class) > presence of suppressor (17-28 dB peak) > host action/port noise at the ear (up to about 15 dB loss of benefit on an AR right ear) > can size (about 6 dB long vs short) > wet vs dry (3-10 dB, temporary) > barrel length (about 1-3 dB at the ear, more at the muzzle) > differences between comparable quality cans (under about 3 dB).

### Gaps
- The MIL-STD-1474D/E texts were not read directly; details come from equipment makers, press and journal summaries. One B&K page dates 1474E to 2005, others to 2015 (2015 appears correct).
- PEW Science does not publish the rating's equations or named tier bands in page text (tier chart is an image); numeric peak/impulse tables are images or members-only.
- No USSOCOM or Army Research Laboratory suppressor evaluation report was retrieved.

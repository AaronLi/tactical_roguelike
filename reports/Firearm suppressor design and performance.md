# Suppressors tame the blast, never the crack

A suppressor is a muzzle-mounted expansion chamber that traps, cools and slows propellant gas so the pressure step released into open air is far smaller; it typically removes **20-35 dB of muzzle blast** (instrumented range 3-35 dB) and does nothing to the supersonic bullet's own shock wave. A suppressed shot is therefore still loud: about **116-130 dB peak at 1 m for the quietest subsonic systems** and **130-140 dB for centrefire rifles**, against 157-167 dB for unsuppressed service pistols and rifles. Three things move the result more than the brand of suppressor: the cartridge and whether it is subsonic, the host weapon (barrel length and whether the action vents gas near the shooter), and the size of the can. Modern designs divide into conventional gas-trapping baffle stacks, which are quietest at the muzzle, and flow-through or low-back-pressure designs, which give up a little suppression so that gas-operated guns keep running normally; 3D printing, the 1.375x24 "HUB" mount and general military issue define the current generation. The practical effect of suppression is less about silence than about information: listeners near the bullet's path still hear a roughly 148 dB crack, but lose the muzzle "thump" that tells them where the shooter is. One caution applies to every number below: decibel figures depend on microphone position, weighting and metric, so values from different sources are only roughly comparable, and differences under about 3 dB are noise.

## A gunshot is three sounds, and a suppressor only quiets one

A gunshot has separable sources: the muzzle blast from propellant gas, the N-shaped ballistic shock wave of a supersonic bullet, and the noise of a cycling action ([NIOSH, Murphy et al. 2018](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/221679/cdc_221679_DS1.pdf)). A US Navy briefing calls this "crack-thump": the crack is the bullet's shock wave, the thump is the muzzle blast ([NSWC Crane 2009](https://ndia.dtic.mil/wp-content/uploads/2009/infantrysmallarms/thursdaysessionxi8453.pdf)). The blast lasts about 3-5 ms, is strongest in the direction the barrel points and weakens off-axis ([Maher 2006](https://fdn.montana.edu/rmaher/publications/maher_aac_0406.pdf)). The crack is generated outside the weapon along the whole supersonic part of the flight path, which is why no muzzle device can touch it ([Dater 2014](https://www.larsondavis.com/ContentStore/mktg/LD_Docs/Firearm_Sound_Briefing.pdf)).

The blast itself comes from "uncorking": the sudden release of high-pressure gas when the bullet leaves the bore. A suppressor lowers that pressure before release in two ways, by giving the gas more volume and cooling it, and by stretching the release over a longer time through trapping and turbulence ([Dater 2014](https://www.larsondavis.com/ContentStore/mktg/LD_Docs/Firearm_Sound_Briefing.pdf)). Structurally it is a tube of expansion chambers separated by baffles, each with a bore hole slightly larger than the bullet, usually behind a larger first "blast chamber"; most designs have roughly 4 to 15 chambers ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silencer_(firearms))). The scale of the job is large. A 5.56 rifle with a 15-inch barrel uncorks at about **7,900 psi**, and pressure inside the suppressor's first chamber is about **2,000 psi on a 14.5-inch barrel versus 3,000 psi on a 10.5-inch barrel** ([Dater & Wong 2010](https://ndia.dtic.mil/wp-content/uploads/2010/armament/WednesdayCumberlandPhilipDater.pdf)). Unsuppressed loudness tracks uncorking pressure almost perfectly, so short barrels are both louder and harder on suppressors ([Dater 2014](https://www.larsondavis.com/ContentStore/mktg/LD_Docs/Firearm_Sound_Briefing.pdf)).

The crack is the limit of what suppression can do. Measured 1 m beside the flight path, it was essentially identical with and without a suppressor: .308 at 151.9 versus 152.6 dB, 5.56 M855 at 148.1 versus 148.5 dB ([Dater 2014](https://www.larsondavis.com/ContentStore/mktg/LD_Docs/Firearm_Sound_Briefing.pdf)). Only keeping the bullet below the local speed of sound removes it, and that speed moves with temperature: **1,051 ft/s at 0 F, 1,087 at 32 F, 1,118 at 60 F, 1,139 at 80 F and 1,160 at 100 F** ([Dater 2014](https://www.larsondavis.com/ContentStore/mktg/LD_Docs/Firearm_Sound_Briefing.pdf)). Factory subsonic loads run about 1,000-1,050 ft/s with shot-to-shot spreads of up to 50 ft/s, so a load that is safely subsonic in warm weather can crack intermittently in the cold ([American Rifleman](https://www.americanrifleman.org/content/subsonic-ammo-101-everything-the-suppressor-shooter-should-know/)). Humidity shifts the speed of sound by only about 4 ft/s ([TFB](https://www.thefirearmblog.com/blog/2024/06/01/suppressor-sound-science/)). The cost of going subsonic is energy: dropping .308 from about 2,700 to 950 ft/s cuts muzzle energy by a factor of about 8, which is why purpose-built heavy-bullet cartridges such as .300 Blackout (190-220 gr at about 1,020-1,070 ft/s) and 9x39 exist ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silencer_(firearms))).

Action noise is the third source and the one a suppressor can make worse. On self-loading guns the gas vented at the ejection port "can become the dominant noise source for the shooter", and the suppressor does not reduce it ([NIOSH](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/221679/cdc_221679_DS1.pdf)). Mechanical noise also sets a floor: an AR-15 bolt slamming shut measures about 117-123 dB peak at 1 m, within a few decibels of a good suppressed .22 shot ([SADJ](https://sadefensejournal.com/dr-daters-lmo-suppressor-class/2); [Bookstaber](https://david.bookstaber.com/Interests/2017/08/introduction-to-shots-pops-and-sound-pressure-levels)).

Two usage effects round out the mechanism. **First round pop** is the louder first shot through a suppressor full of fresh air: oxygen in the can supports extra combustion, and later shots fire into oxygen-depleted gas until a pause lets air back in ([NIOSH](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/221679/cdc_221679_DS1.pdf)). Independent data express it as hazard, not decibels: on subsonic .300 Blackout the first shot carried over 280% more peak impulse than follow-ups ([PEW Science 6.88](https://pewscience.com/sound-signature-reviews-free/sss-6-88-ddc-enticer-l-minifix-300blk)), and on a short 5.56 carbine first-shot bystander hazard was over twice that of later shots ([PEW Science 6.151](https://pewscience.com/sound-signature-reviews-free/sss-6-151-surefire-556rc3-mk18-556)). No citable decibel figure for first round pop was found, so any game value is a design choice. Firing "wet" (a little water or gel in the can) cools the gas and displaces air; trade sources put the gain at about **3-10 dB for the first several shots**, mostly useful with pistol and subsonic loads, and there is no industry standard for measuring it ([RECOIL](https://www.recoilweb.com/suppressor-enhancement-making-a-can-quieter-173840.html); [SADJ](https://sadefensejournal.com/dr-daters-lmo-suppressor-class/2)).

## Cartridge, host and can size outweigh the brand of suppressor

Ammunition is the largest lever. Suppressor reductions are larger with low-velocity than high-velocity ammunition in the same guns, because supersonic bullets add shock waves the suppressor cannot affect ([Murphy et al. 2018](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/211059)). Cartridge energy sets the baseline: one modular suppressor's manufacturer figures run from 123.3 dB on 9 mm to 130.6 on 5.56, 136.9 on .308 and 141.4 on .338 Lapua, an 18 dB spread from the cartridge alone ([Silencer Central](https://www.silencercentral.com/products/silencerco-omega-36m)). .22 LR and .45 ACP are the classic easy cases, being low-pressure and subsonic ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silencer_(firearms))).

The host weapon matters about as much as the can. With the same suppressor and ammunition, measured 1 m left of the muzzle relative to a 14.5-inch M4, an HK416 was **+5 dB**, a Steyr AUG **+9 dB** and a SIG 551 **+14 dB**, while a 20-inch bolt action was equal and a 20-inch AR-15 was 2 dB quieter; piston systems vent gas ahead of the suppressor where it cannot be treated ([Dater 2014](https://www.larsondavis.com/ContentStore/mktg/LD_Docs/Firearm_Sound_Briefing.pdf)). At the shooter's head the action dominates. On AR-15s, suppressors cut peaks 20-25 dB at 1 m from the muzzle and 15-25 dB at the left ear, but only **6-9 dB at the right ear**, beside the ejection port ([NIOSH](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/221679/cdc_221679_DS1.pdf)). Bolt actions reverse this: on a 20-inch .308 the shooter's ear averaged about 8.9 dBA quieter than the muzzle-side reference position ([Precision Rifle Blog](https://precisionrifleblog.com/2024/09/14/2024-suppressor-summit-hard-data-to-compare-250-suppresors/)). Manually operated actions are unaffected in function and produce no blowback because the breech stays closed ([B&T](https://bt-ag.ch/en/know-how/blowback-backpressure-rbs/)). Simple blowback guns are the worst hosts for the shooter, venting sooty gas rearward through the ejection port ([TFB](https://www.thefirearmblog.com/blog/low-back-pressure-suppressors-do-they-really-make-a-difference-44817520)).

Barrel length shows up more in blast energy than in peak decibels. Manufacturer at-ear data for one 5.56 can show 137.74 dB on a 10.3-inch barrel against 136.40 dB on a 14.5-inch barrel, a gap of only 1.3 dB ([TFB](https://www.thefirearmblog.com/blog/2023/12/16/surefire-socom556-rc3/)). Yet an unsuppressed 10.3-inch 5.56 produces a primary blast similar in peak to a 20-inch 7.62x51, plus an afterburning secondary blast that makes its impulse almost 30% more severe ([PEW Science 6.51](https://pewscience.com/sound-signature-reviews-free/sss-6-51-research-supplement-unsuppressed-rifle-and-mk18)). Suppressor rankings also do not transfer cleanly between barrel lengths ([PEW Science 6.127](https://pewscience.com/sound-signature-reviews-free/sss-6-127-research-supplement-unsuppressed-rifle-and-m4a1)).

On the suppressor side, internal volume and how well the baffles delay and cool gas dominate; baffle shape matters less than marketing suggests. Four contemporary 5.56 duty cans from different makers all metered around 135 dB, within 3 dB of each other, and day-to-day atmospheric variation was enough to reorder six brands ([SADJ](https://sadefensejournal.com/dr-daters-lmo-suppressor-class/2)). Size is the visible lever. One modular can on a .300 Win Mag read 132.7 dB long and 138.7 dB short at the ear, a 6 dB gap ([American Rifleman](https://www.americanrifleman.org/content/tested-silencerco-omega-36m-suppressor/)); on a 6.5 Creedmoor, a maker's 9-inch model read 135.2 dB and its 5-inch sibling 147.4 dB, a 12 dB gap ([Outdoor Life](https://www.outdoorlife.com/gear/suppressor-recoil-test.md)). An oversized bore, such as a .30 can on a 5.56 rifle, lowers the muzzle-side result but also lowers back pressure, which slightly improves the result at the shooter's ear on a gas gun ([PEW Science 6.143](https://pewscience.com/sound-signature-reviews-free/sss-6-143-ocl-polonium-30-mk18-556)). Materials affect weight and heat durability, not sound.

Back pressure is the central trade-off. A suppressor does not raise peak pressure but lengthens the pressure curve, giving a gas-operated action surplus energy; a visible sign is AR ejection angle moving from about 120-130 degrees unsuppressed to 45-50 degrees with a conventional can and about 90 degrees with a reduced-back-pressure can ([B&T](https://bt-ag.ch/en/know-how/blowback-backpressure-rbs/)). The consequences are a faster bolt, a higher cyclic rate, faster carbon fouling, lower reliability in dust and humidity, and gas in the shooter's face, which is worse indoors and under night vision ([TFB](https://www.thefirearmblog.com/blog/low-back-pressure-suppressors-do-they-really-make-a-difference-44817520)). How much the cyclic rate rises is contested: one expert cites up to 200 rounds per minute ([SADJ](https://sadefensejournal.com/dr-daters-lmo-suppressor-class/2)), manufacturer tests show about 847 rpm rising to about 1,097 rpm with a conventional can ([Athlon Outdoors](https://athlonoutdoors.com/article/pof-rifles-maxflo-3d-suppressors/)), and one small independent machine-gun test found only 10-22 rpm of difference ([TFB](https://www.thefirearmblog.com/blog/silencer-saturday-402-suppressor-impact-on-machine-gun-rate-of-fire-44823388)).

Measurement method changes the headline number. The industry convention is a microphone 1 m to the left of the muzzle; the shooter's-ear position is about 15 cm from the ear ([PEW Science](https://pewscience.com/silencer-sound-standard-test-method)). The same suppressed .22 pistol shot read 122.2 dBA, 118.8 dBC and 126.0 dB unweighted ([Dater 2014](https://www.larsondavis.com/ContentStore/mktg/LD_Docs/Firearm_Sound_Briefing.pdf)). Peak and energy metrics also disagree: the same suppressors cut peak level at the ear by 17-24 dB but 8-hour equivalent energy by only 9-21 dB ([Murphy et al. 2018](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/211059)). Some vendor figures in circulation are simply wrong, including claims of suppressed supersonic .308 at 165-170 dB and suppressed subsonic fire "as low as about 70 dB"; both contradict every instrumented measurement here.

## Modern designs split between trapping gas and letting it flow

The lineage explains today's categories. Hiram Percy Maxim's commercial silencer was patented in 1909; the Second World War produced integrally suppressed clandestine weapons such as the High Standard HDM, the Welrod and the De Lisle carbine; the Cold War added systems built around subsonic ammunition (MP5SD, PBS-1, AS Val and VSS) and captive-piston cartridges that trap gas in the case and need no suppressor at all ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silencer_(firearms)); [TFB](https://www.thefirearmblog.com/blog/the-soviet-pss-pistol-and-the-rise-of-captive-piston-ammunition-guns-44826727)). The key technical shift was from consumables to metal. Elastomer wipes that the bullet punches through typically last about five shots and hurt accuracy; steel-wool packing degrades within about ten; modern all-metal baffle stacks have no wear parts, and the best rifle suppressors claim service lives above 30,000 rounds ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silencer_(firearms))). The Soviet PBS-1 sits between the eras, combining baffles with a rubber wipe that needs replacing after roughly 20-25 rounds ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.com/wiki/PBS-1_silencer)).

Baffle families differ in shape and in whether the stack is separate parts or one piece. Cone baffles are the long-established baseline, tall and steep in rifle suppressors and flatter in pistol ones; the K-baffle, a flat disc joined to a cone, has been an industry standard since the 1980s; a monocore replaces the stack with a single machined core ([US patent 8,100,224](https://uspto.report/patent/grant/8100224); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silencer_(firearms))). Rifle suppressors are usually sealed and fully welded, while rimfire suppressors are built to come apart for cleaning ([TFB](https://www.thefirearmblog.com/blog/2018/10/11/dead-air-silencers-nomad/); [American Rifleman](https://www.americanrifleman.org/articles/2015/11/30/review-dead-air-armament-mask-hd-sound-suppressor/)).

The defining split of the 2020s is gas management. PEW Science sorts current designs into **conventional** cans, which reflect the early blast rearward and restrict later flow; **flow-through** designs (HUXWRX), which vent gas forward through secondary paths; and hybrids such as SIG's "Forward Flux", CAT's "Surge Bypass", CGS Hyperion and the SureFire RC2 family. Its stated trade-off is that higher flow lowers back pressure but generally lowers suppression, particularly with subsonic ammunition ([PEW Science 6.124](https://pewscience.com/sound-signature-reviews-free/sss-6-124-research-supplement-flow-through-forward-flux-surge-bypass-conventional-300-blk-rifle-silencers)). Flow-through cans also tend to flash more ([Pew Pew Tactical](https://www.pewpewtactical.com/flow-through-suppressors/)). The pattern at the shooter's position can invert: bypass designs are often louder at the muzzle but quieter at the ear, because less gas exits the ejection port ([B&T](https://bt-ag.ch/en/know-how/blowback-backpressure-rbs/)). SureFire's SOCOM556-RC3 illustrates the premium: a claimed 60% less back pressure than the RC2 in the same envelope, at $1,799 against $1,199 ([TFB](https://cdn-fastly.thefirearmblog.com/blog/2023/12/09/surefire-socom-rc3/)); one reviewer judged it about 2 dB louder by ear on a 14.5-inch host ([TFB](https://www.thefirearmblog.com/blog/2023/12/16/surefire-socom556-rc3/)). Low back pressure matters most on short gas-operated rifles and not at all on bolt actions, where a high-back-pressure can costs nothing.

Additive manufacturing is tied to that trend. Laser powder-bed printing builds a suppressor as one piece with no welds, makes all-Inconel bodies practical where Inconel was once confined to the blast baffle, and allows internal flow channels and porous cores that cannot be machined ([Gun Digest](https://gundigest.com/gear-ammo/suppressors/3d-printed-suppressor)). Material choice maps to duty rating. Aluminium loses strength near 250 F and titanium by about 600 F, while Inconel 718 holds strength to roughly 1,300-1,400 F, so titanium suits light weight and low rates of fire and stainless or Inconel suits short barrels and heavy fire ([TFB](https://www.thefirearmblog.com/blog/2024/05/18/suppressor-alloy-metallurgy/)). Makers sell the same printed model in a light titanium version that is not full-auto rated and a heavier Inconel version that is ([Gun Digest](https://gundigest.com/gear-ammo/suppressors/3d-printed-suppressor)). "Full-auto rated" has no common definition and depends on each maker's firing schedule ([MeatEater](https://www.themeateater.com/hunt/firearm-hunting/all-your-suppressor-questions-answered)).

Integral suppressors are built around the barrel as part of the weapon. The MP5SD ports its barrel so standard supersonic 9 mm leaves subsonic, at a cost in velocity; the AS Val and VSS instead pair an integral suppressor with the heavy subsonic 9x39 cartridge ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silencer_(firearms))). The current trend is the matched system: a short-barrelled weapon sold with a dedicated suppressor, such as SOCOM's Suppressed Upper Receiver Group, a complete upper designed to be fired continuously suppressed ([Military Times](https://www.militarytimes.com/off-duty/gearscout/2018/08/13/socom-is-buying-this-new-suppressed-upper-receiver-group-to-make-its-troops-quieter-and-deadlier/)).

Mounting has consolidated around one open standard. The 1.375x24 "HUB" socket in the rear of the suppressor accepts direct-thread adapters, any brand's quick-detach system, a three-lug mount or a pistol piston ([TFB](https://www.thefirearmblog.com/blog/2022/11/12/1-375-x-24-universal-mount-explained/)). SureFire's SOCOM fast-attach interface is the major closed ecosystem ([Rifle Configurator](https://www.rifleconfigurator.com/guides/suppressor-mounting-systems)). Tilting-barrel pistols need a spring-loaded piston, or Nielsen device, that briefly decouples the suppressor's mass so the action can cycle; fixed-barrel guns do not ([SilencerCo](https://silencerco.com/blog/what-is-nielsen-device)).

Suppressors have also moved from special-operations kit to general issue. The US Marine Corps began widespread fielding to infantry units in December 2020, targeting about 30,000 suppressors by fiscal 2023 ([MARCORSYSCOM](https://www.marcorsyscom.marines.mil/News/Article/2458429/marine-corps-begins-widespread-fielding-of-suppressors/)), after experiments found they significantly improved command and control ([TFB](https://cdn-fastly.thefirearmblog.com/blog/2021/01/13/usmc-suppressor-roll-out/)). The US Army's M7 rifle is issued with a suppressor as part of the system, weighing 8.38 lb bare and 9.84 lb suppressed ([The War Zone](https://www.twz.com/land/sig-sauers-m7-rifle-gets-official-army-seal-of-approval-despite-controversy)). Military designs now sell on flash, toxic fumes and reliability as much as sound: SIG's SLX line claims 70-80% fume reduction ([SIG Sauer](https://www.sigsauer.com/blog/sig-sauer-introduces-the-next-generation-of-suppressor-technology-with-slx-and-slh-series-of-suppressors)).

The **140 dB peak** line is the usual "hearing safe" threshold, shared by OSHA and NIOSH as the ceiling for impulse noise ([OSHA](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.95)). Rimfire, subsonic pistol-calibre guns, subsonic .300 Blackout and full-size cans on bolt-action rifles land under it; short cans, magnums and short 5.56 carbines at the ear sit at or above it. NIOSH's authors conclude that suppressed firearms still pose a significant hearing risk ([Murphy et al. 2018](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/211059)). For scale, a hammer driving a nail measures 133 dB peak at 1 m ([Bookstaber](https://david.bookstaber.com/Interests/2017/08/introduction-to-shots-pops-and-sound-pressure-levels)) and a BB gun 97 dB ([Silencer Central](https://www.silencercentral.com/blog/how-loud-is-a-gunshot-gun-db-levels-compared/)). Historic figures need care: the 85.5 dB cited for the De Lisle carbine ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silencer_(firearms))) cannot be compared with modern peak measurements, in which a bolt closing alone reads 117 dB. Captive-piston ammunition measured 124.6 dB, about level with an integrally suppressed .22 pistol ([SADJ](https://sadefensejournal.com/dr-daters-lmo-suppressor-class/2)).

PEW Science's Suppression Rating offers an alternative to decibels: a 0-100 hearing-hazard score, higher being quieter, computed from full pressure waveforms at the muzzle and the shooter's ear for one specific gun, suppressor and ammunition ([PEW Science](https://pewscience.com/silencer-sound-standard-suppression-rating)). Published examples span about 22 to 65.

### The crack holds near 148 dB while the blast fades 6 dB per doubling

The crack measures about **144-153 dB peak at 1 m from the flight path** ([Dater 2014](https://www.larsondavis.com/ContentStore/mktg/LD_Docs/Firearm_Sound_Briefing.pdf)). Because it is regenerated along the trajectory, it barely fades with range from the shooter: a 5.56 rifle bullet produced 150.1, 151.3, 147.2 and 148.4 dB(C) beside the line of fire at 50, 100, 200 and 300 m ([Paddan & Lower 2016](https://generic.wordpress.soton.ac.uk/isvr-new/wp-content/uploads/sites/422/2022/07/SA80-manuscript.pdf)). How fast the crack decays with sideways distance from the trajectory was not found in any source. Muzzle blast, by contrast, falls close to 6 dB per doubling of distance.

| Distance to the side of an unsuppressed 5.56 rifle | Peak level, dB(C) |
|---|---|
| 0.3 m | 161.2 |
| 1 m | 158.2 |
| 2 m | 155.5 |
| 4 m | 152.9 |
| 8 m | 143.9 |
| 16 m | 136.0 |
| 32 m | 128.1 |

Source: [Paddan & Lower 2016](https://generic.wordpress.soton.ac.uk/isvr-new/wp-content/uploads/sites/422/2022/07/SA80-manuscript.pdf).

The arithmetic that follows is useful. Six decibels is a doubling of sound pressure, so a 30 dB reduction makes a shot at 1 m as loud as an unsuppressed shot at about 32 m, and shrinks the free-field distance to any given loudness by the same factor of about 32. Real terrain, atmospheric absorption and background noise reduce that advantage at long range. A US Army Research Laboratory report models detection ranges for suppressed and unsuppressed M4 fire, but its tables could not be retrieved ([ARL-TR-9277](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/trecms/pdf/AD1147939.pdf)); measured real-world audibility distances for suppressed fire remain the largest gap in this research.

Localisation is where suppression pays off. The crack arrives first and points at the bullet's path; the thump locates the shooter, and soldiers are trained to ignore the crack and wait for the thump ([NSWC Crane 2009](https://ndia.dtic.mil/wp-content/uploads/2009/infantrysmallarms/thursdaysessionxi8453.pdf)). The delay between them grows from 94 ms at 50 m to 507 ms at 300 m ([Paddan & Lower 2016](https://generic.wordpress.soton.ac.uk/isvr-new/wp-content/uploads/sites/422/2022/07/SA80-manuscript.pdf)). Suppressor reduction is greatest to the side, smaller to the rear, and insignificant within about 50 degrees of the line of fire with supersonic ammunition ([NIOSH](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/221679/cdc_221679_DS1.pdf)). A listener downrange therefore still hears the crack but loses the cue to the shooter's bearing; one widely repeated claim is that observers misjudge the shooter's position by 90 to 180 degrees, though that figure traces only to an unverified encyclopedia entry ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silencer_(firearms))).

The recoil data expose a second trade-off: the quietest cans reduce recoil least, and brake-capped cans that halve recoil give up 10-25 dB. Heat has tactical consequences beyond durability. A hot suppressor produces mirage over the sight line after as few as 2-3 shots in the right conditions ([SilencerCo](https://silencerco.com/?p=1192170)), and the M7's improved thermal shield delays the visible heat signature under night vision to about 100 rounds from about 40 ([The War Zone](https://www.twz.com/land/sig-sauers-m7-rifle-gets-official-army-seal-of-approval-despite-controversy)). Suppressors also reduce muzzle flash by containing and cooling the gas, but no measured flash-reduction figure was found ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silencer_(firearms))).

## Conclusion

The evidence argues against the simplest game model, a fixed decibel bonus per suppressor. Performance belongs to the system of weapon, suppressor and ammunition, and it splits into two outputs that often move in opposite directions: the muzzle signature heard by others, driven by cartridge, barrel length and can size, and the shooter's own exposure, driven by action type and back pressure. A faithful model has three emitters. Muzzle blast sits at the gun, is louder forward, falls 6 dB per doubling of distance, and is the only term the suppressor scales. The crack exists only for supersonic ammunition, runs along the bullet's path at roughly constant strength, and reveals that shots are being fired without revealing from where. Action noise sits at the gun, is untouched by the suppressor, and sets the floor for semi-automatics. Suppressed supersonic fire then becomes "detected but not located", and suppressed subsonic fire becomes short-range, low-damage and close to unidentifiable, which matches how the real trade-offs work.

The attachment design space is also richer than quiet versus loud. The sources support at least four distinct archetypes: conventional cans (quietest, with penalties on gas guns and none on bolt actions), flow-through cans (slightly louder and flashier, no host penalty), compact cans (6-12 dB worse for less length and weight) and brake-capped cans (a recoil device that is barely a suppressor). Material adds a weight-versus-sustained-fire axis, and first round pop, wet firing, cold-weather subsonic failures and heat mirage are all real mechanics with sourced direction, even where the exact magnitude is not published. Where the research is thin (first-round-pop decibels, flash reduction, real-terrain detection range, crack decay with miss distance) the numbers are design choices and should be treated as tunable, not as reference values.
